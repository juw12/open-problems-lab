A C4-free subgraph of the 8-cube has at most 690 edges:
a Lean-checked parity argument

Juwen Lie

Independent; ORCID 0009-0008-1384-7901  ·  25 November 2026  ·  version 3 (Thomason and Wagner [7] read: a table in their paper gives f(8) ≤ 689, Section 5; the theorem and its proof are unchanged)
Archived with the Lean files: doi:10.5281/zenodo.24338429 (all versions of github.com/juw12/open-problems-lab; version 1 of this paper is in release v1.1.0, doi:10.5281/zenodo.24522415, version 2 in v1.1.1, doi:10.5281/zenodo.24523255)

Abstract. Let f(n) be the largest number of edges of a subgraph of the n-dimensional cube Qn that contains no 4-cycle. We show f(8) ≤ 690. The proof combines a star-capacity count that goes back to Chung (1992) and is used in this form by Zhou, Ma, Yang, Ren, Yang and Li, which gives 691, with a parity count inside the 16 facets that rules 691 out. The statement and its proof were checked by the Lean 4 kernel with Mathlib. With the known construction on 682 edges this gives 682 ≤ f(8) ≤ 690. The proof was produced by an AI agent and has not been read by a mathematician. The bound 690 is not the best one that follows from published work: a table printed by Thomason and Wagner (2009), the result of a computer enumeration in the 5-cube, gives f(8) ≤ 689 by averaging over sub-5-cubes, although the number is not stated in their paper (Section 5). What this paper adds is a proof of 690 that uses no computer search and has been checked by a proof assistant.
How this was made, and what to trust. I am a mechanical engineer, not a mathematician. The statement, the proof below and its Lean formalisation were written by an AI agent (a large language model) in a research lab that I run on my own PC; I did not find the argument myself. What can be trusted is narrow and exact: the Lean 4 kernel accepted a proof of the formal statement in Section 4, using only Lean's three standard axioms. What has not been checked by a person: that the argument is new, and that the formal definitions say what the words say (they are short, and printed in Section 4 so that a reader can judge). The bound itself is not the best available; Section 5 says what is. A pointer to an earlier source is the most useful reply to this paper.

1. The question and what is known

The vertices of Qn are the 0/1 words of length n; two are joined when they differ in exactly one coordinate. Erdős asked how large a subgraph of Qn without a 4-cycle can be (problem 86 on erdosproblems.com [1]). Write f(n) for the maximum. The asymptotic question is open. For small n the values f(4) = 24, f(5) = 56 and f(6) = 132 have been known for decades, and f(7) = 304 was proved on 20 November 2026 by Zhou, Ma, Yang, Ren, Yang and Li [2]. For n = 8 we know of the following bounds.

BoundSource
f(8) ≥ 682a construction from the fully frustrated ground state of Marinari, Parisi and Ritort; edge list in Minamoto [3]
f(8) ≤ 694averaging over the 16 facets from f(7) = 304: 7·e ≤ 16·304
f(8) ≤ 691star capacity (Lemmas 4 and 5 of Chung [6]; Lemma 2.2 of [2]) with C(d,3) ≥ 10d − 40; Step 1 and Step 2 below. The number is not printed in [6] or [2]
f(8) ≤ 690this paper; no computer search, checked by Lean
f(8) ≤ 689follows from Table 1 of Thomason and Wagner [7], a computer enumeration in Q5, by averaging over the 448 sub-5-cubes (Section 5). The number is not printed in [7]; we did not re-compute the table
Theorem. Every subgraph of Q8 without a 4-cycle has at most 690 edges.

So 682 ≤ f(8) ≤ 690 with a proof that the Lean kernel has checked, and 682 ≤ f(8) ≤ 689 if Table 1 of [7] is accepted. The gap is not closed, and Minamoto [3] lists the exact value of ex(Q8, C4), and whether it is 682, as open.

2. Notation

Q8 has 256 vertices and 1024 edges. Let P be the set of the 128 vertices with an even number of ones; every edge has exactly one end in P. A 3-face is a 3-dimensional subcube: a set J of three free coordinates and fixed values on the other five. There are C(8,3)·25 = 1792 of them. A facet is a set {x : xc = b}; there are 16, each is a copy of Q7, and each contains C(7,3)·24 = 560 of the 3-faces. For a subgraph G, a star in a 3-face F is a vertex v of P in F such that all three edges of F at v are in G. We write d(v) for the degree of v in G.

3. Proof

Suppose G is a subgraph of Q8 without a 4-cycle and with at least 691 edges. Delete edges until G has exactly 691; it still has no 4-cycle.

Step 1 (star capacity; Lemmas 4 and 5 of [6], Lemma 2.2 of [2]). A 3-face holds four vertices of P, and any two of them have two common neighbours inside the face. If two of them were stars, the four edges from these two vertices to their two common neighbours would form a 4-cycle. So each 3-face carries at most one star. A vertex of degree d is a star in exactly C(d,3) different 3-faces, one for each choice of three of its edges. Hence

S := Σv∈P C(d(v), 3) = number of 3-faces that carry a star ≤ 1792.

Step 2 (every degree on P is 5 or 6). Put w(d) = C(d,3) − 10d + 40. For d = 0, 1, …, 8 its values are

40, 30, 20, 11, 4, 0, 0, 5, 16,

so w(5) = w(6) = 0 and w(d) ≥ 4 for every other d. The degrees over P add up to 691, the number of edges. So

S = 10·691 − 40·128 + Σv∈P w(d(v)) = 1790 + Σv∈P w(d(v)) ≤ 1792.

The sum of the w(d(v)) is at most 2, and each term is 0 or at least 4, so every term is 0. Every vertex of P has degree 5 or 6, and S = 1790. (With 692 edges the same line reads 1800 ≤ 1792, which is the bound f(8) ≤ 691.)

Step 3 (exactly two 3-faces carry no star). 1792 − 1790 = 2. Call them F1 ≠ F2.

Step 4 (facet parity). Let K be a facet {x : xc = b}. A vertex v of P in K has degree d(v) or d(v) − 1 inside K, that is 4, 5 or 6, and C(4,3) = 4, C(5,3) = 10, C(6,3) = 20 are all even. So the number of (vertex, 3-face) pairs with the 3-face inside K and the vertex a star in it is even. By Step 1 these stars sit in different 3-faces, so the number of 3-faces inside K that carry a star is even. K contains 560 3-faces, an even number. Therefore the number of star-less 3-faces inside K is even: K contains none or both of F1, F2.

Step 5 (two different 3-faces are separated by a facet). Write F1 = (J1, b1) and F2 = (J2, b2). If J1 ≠ J2, choose a coordinate c in J2 and not in J1. If J1 = J2, the fixed values differ at some coordinate c outside J1. In both cases the facet K = {x : xc = b1(c)} contains F1 and does not contain F2. This K holds exactly one star-less 3-face, against Step 4. So G does not exist. ∎

Remarks. (a) Step 4 uses that 4, 10 and 20 are all even. In Q7 the local degrees would be 3, 4, 5 with C(d,3) = 1, 4, 10, of mixed parity, so the argument has no analogue there; this fits the fact that f(7) = 304 needed the much longer proof of [2]. (b) For 690 edges the count of Step 2 leaves a slack of 12 instead of 2, and some configurations pass the parity test, for example four star-less 3-faces with the same free coordinates whose fixed values form an affine plane. Reaching 689 this way needs another idea; Section 5 gets 689 from the computation of [7]. (c) Averaging over the 18 facets of Q9 gives 8·f(9) ≤ 18·f(8), hence f(9) ≤ 1552 (from 694 one gets 1561). The table of [7] gives f(9) ≤ 1549.

4. The formal statement and the machine check

The proof was formalised in Lean 4 (v4.32.0) with Mathlib. The file Q8Parity.lean stands alone (it imports Mathlib and nothing else), has 56 declarations and ends with the theorem below. The lab's checker compiles the file and prints the axioms the theorem depends on; they are Lean's three standard ones (propext, Classical.choice, Quot.sound), with no sorry and no native_decide. The definitions, in ASCII (the file has the exact text):

abbrev V (n : Nat) := Fin n -> Bool                       -- vertices of Q_n
def Adj (x y : V n) : Prop := hamDist x y = 1              -- differ in exactly one coordinate
def IsEdge (e : Finset (V n)) : Prop := exists x y, Adj x y /\ e = {x, y}
def HasC4 (H : Finset (Finset (V n))) : Prop :=
  exists a b c d, a != c /\ b != d /\ {a, b} in H /\ {b, c} in H /\ {c, d} in H /\ {d, a} in H
def Bound (n M : Nat) : Prop :=
  forall H : Finset (Finset (V n)), (forall e in H, IsEdge e) -> not (HasC4 H) -> H.card <= M
theorem bound_8_690 : Bound 8 690

The Lean proof follows Steps 1 to 5 but counts uncovered (direction, vertex) pairs, 16 of them, instead of naming the two faces. Two checks that the formal statement is neither empty nor too strong: the same general inequality (Stars.edge_bound in Stars.lean) proves f(4) ≤ 24 and f(5) ≤ 56, which are the exact values, and f(6) ≤ 133 and f(7) ≤ 306 against the true 132 and 304; and 690 lies above the 682 construction. The kernel checks that the proof proves this statement. It does not check that the definitions are the right reading of “C4-free subgraph of the cube”.

5. Earlier work, and what is not known

A table of Thomason and Wagner [7] gives 689. Their paper bounds the limit of f(n)/e(Qn) by 0.62256 and states no bound for n = 7 or 8. Its Table 1 lists the 33 extreme points of the convex hull of the vectors (s1, …, s5) of all C4-free subgraphs H of Q5, where sr = Σv C(dH(v), r) counts the stars with r edges. The table is the result of a computer enumeration that took about 17 hours. On each of the 33 points one checks by hand that

12·s1 − 5·s2 + 11·s4 − 20·s5 ≤ 800,     (∗)

with equality at four of them, among them (112, 144, 80, 16, 0), the graph with 56 edges. So (∗) holds for every C4-free subgraph of Q5. Now let G be C4-free in Q8. There are C(8,5)·23 = 448 sub-5-cubes, and a star with r edges lies in C(8−r, 5−r) of them, that is in 35, 20, 10, 4, 1 for r = 1, …, 5. Adding (∗) over the sub-5-cubes gives

Σv h(d(v)) ≤ 800·448 = 358 400,    h(d) = 420·d − 100·C(d,2) + 44·C(d,4) − 20·C(d,5).

For d = 0, 1, …, 8 the values of h(d) − 260·d are 0, 160, 220, 180, 84, 0, 0, 140, 440, never negative. The degrees add up to 2e, so 520·e ≤ 358 400, e ≤ 8960/13 = 689.23…, and f(8) ≤ 689.

Three cautions. This derivation was made by an AI agent and has not been read by a mathematician. We did not find the number 689 in [7] or elsewhere, and we do not know whether it is written down somewhere. And it rests on Table 1, which we copied from the paper and did not re-compute; a direct check of (∗) by integer programming, without the table, is not finished (a 45-second run found the value 800 and proved only ≤ 812). The same table gives f(5) ≤ 56, f(6) ≤ 133, f(7) ≤ 306, f(9) ≤ 1549 and f(10) ≤ 3398; the scripts thomason_wagner_certificate.py and q5_inequality_ilp.py are in the folder prize_work/flagship-erdos-86-f7/sources of the repository.

So the position is this. The bound 690 has a proof of one page with no computer search, checked by Lean (this paper). The bound 689 follows from a computation of 2009 that we have not reproduced.

Whether 690 was written down before. We found no upper bound for f(8) in [2], in [3], in the OEIS entry A245762 [4] or on the page of problem 86 [1], all read on 25 November 2026; the full text of [2] and [3] was searched for the numbers 683 and 688 to 696, with no hit. The flag-algebra papers [8] give asymptotic densities only.

What is in Chung's paper [6]. Her Lemmas 4 and 5 hold for every n and say that Σv C(d(v), 3) ≤ 2·C(n,3)·2n−3, the sum over all vertices. This is Step 1 for both parity classes together, so Step 1 is hers, and the bound 691 follows from her paper in two lines although the number is not written there. Her Lemma 11, if it is read as an exact inequality, gives exactly 690 for n = 8. It is not exact, and it is not meant to be: it is a step in a bound for large n, and its proof uses Goodman's bound on monochromatic triangles in the form that holds for large sets. The printed inequality fails in Q4 for the forest with 14 edges made of the two edges {0000, 0001} and {1110, 1111} together with all edges at their four ends: every 3-face has two vertices of degree 3, so the left side of Lemma 11 is 0, while the right side is 12/16. So 690 is not stated in [6] and does not follow from what is proved there. The script prize_work/flagship-erdos-86-f7/chung_check.py in the repository reproduces these numbers.

Not read. We could not obtain Brass, Harborth and Nienborg [5], so an earlier bound may exist there or elsewhere. Only the parity Step 4 may be new, and it is short enough that it may be folklore.

The true value of f(8), and anything about the asymptotic question of problem 86.
